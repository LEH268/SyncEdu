Chapter 1 — Cell Biology and Cell Organisation

1.1 The cell as the basic unit of life

The cell theory states that all living things are made of one or more cells, that the cell is the smallest unit that carries out all life processes, and that every cell arises from a pre-existing cell by division. A unicellular organism such as Amoeba or Paramecium performs every life process within a single cell; a multicellular organism divides the work among specialised cells.

1.2 Animal and plant cells compared

Both animal and plant cells have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and a Golgi apparatus. Plant cells additionally have a cell wall of cellulose, a large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, and chloroplasts containing chlorophyll.

Feature Animal cell Plant cell
Cell wall Absent Present (cellulose)
Chloroplast Absent Present in green parts
Vacuole Small, temporary, many One large permanent central vacuole
Shape Irregular, rounded Fixed, regular

1.3 Organelles and their functions

An organelle is a specialised structure within a cell that performs a particular task, the way an organ does for the whole body.

Organelle Function
Nucleus Contains DNA; controls all cell activities and cell division
Mitochondrion Site of aerobic respiration; releases ATP energy
Chloroplast Absorbs light energy for photosynthesis
Ribosome Site of protein synthesis
Rough endoplasmic reticulum Transports proteins made on its ribosomes
Golgi apparatus Modifies, packages and secretes proteins
Lysosome Contains digestive enzymes to break down worn parts
Vacuole Stores cell sap; keeps a plant cell turgid

Cells that are very active secrete a lot of protein (for example gland cells) have many ribosomes, extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum and many mitochondria; this link between structure and activity is a common exam theme.

1.4 The relationship between organelles

Organelles cooperate. A protein for export is made on a ribosome, threaded into the rough endoplasmic reticulum, carried in a vesicle to the Golgi apparatus for modification, then released at the plasma membrane by exocytosis. The energy for these steps is supplied as ATP from the mitochondria. Describing this pathway in order is worth full marks and shows understanding beyond a list of definitions.

1.5 Levels of cell organisation

In a multicellular organism, structure is built up in stages:

cell → tissue → organ → system → organism

A tissue is a group of similar cells performing the same function, such as muscle tissue or xylem. An organ is several tissues working together for one main function, such as the heart or a leaf. A system is several organs cooperating, such as the digestive system. The organism is the complete living individual.

Example. In a leaf: palisade mesophyll (tissue) for photosynthesis, xylem and phloem (tissues) for transport, and epidermis (tissue) for protection combine to form the leaf (organ), part of the shoot system of the plant (organism).

1.6 Specialised cells

As cells mature they undergo differentiation: they change shape and contents to suit one job, usually losing the ability to do others.

Cell Adaptation
Red blood cell Biconcave, no nucleus — more room for haemoglobin to carry oxygen
Root hair cell Long extension — large surface area to absorb water and minerals
Nerve cell Very long fibre — transmits impulses over distance
Sperm cell Flagellum for swimming; many mitochondria for energy
Palisade cell Many chloroplasts, near the upper leaf surface — maximum light capture

1.7 Cell size and units

Cells are measured in micrometres (µm); 1 mm = 1000 µm. Actual size is found from a drawing using:

actual size = image size ÷ magnification

Small cell size matters because it gives a large surface-area-to-volume ratio, so materials can diffuse in and out fast enough to keep the cell alive. This is why large organisms are made of many small cells rather than a few large ones.

1.8 Density of cell organelles and cell function

Counting organelles under the microscope reveals function: a cell packed with mitochondria is doing energy-hungry work such as active transport in a kidney tubule; a cell full of chloroplasts is photosynthetic; a cell with a huge nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio is often young and dividing. Interpreting such evidence is the skill of linking observed structure to inferred activity.

1.9 Using the light microscope

A light microscope passes light through a thin specimen and magnifies it with two lenses. Total magnification is the eyepiece value multiplied by the objective value, so a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400. Resolution — the ability to tell two close points apart — is limited by the wavelength of light to about 0.2 µm, which is why the electron microscope, using electron beams, is needed to see ribosomes and membranes clearly. When preparing a slide, a drop of stain such as iodine or methylene blue makes colourless structures visible, and the cover slip is lowered at an angle to avoid trapping air bubbles.

1.10 Cell division and continuity

New cells for growth and repair are made by mitosis, in which one parent cell produces two genetically identical daughter cells. This is how a multicellular organism grows from a single fertilised egg and how damaged tissue is replaced. The third part of the cell theory — that every cell comes from a pre-existing cell — is a direct statement that cells never arise spontaneously.

1.11 Density of stomata and gas exchange

Just as organelle counts reveal a cell's job, counting structures across a tissue reveals the tissue's job. The number of stomata per square millimetre of leaf, or of alveoli in a piece of lung, indicates how much gas exchange that tissue supports. Comparing such densities between a sun-adapted and a shade-adapted leaf, or between species, is a standard data-interpretation task that rewards linking a number to a function.
