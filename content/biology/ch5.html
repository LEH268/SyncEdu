Chapter 5 — Nutrition

5.1 Modes of nutrition

Nutrition is the process by which an organism obtains and uses food for energy, growth and repair. Autotrophic organisms such as green plants make their own organic food from carbon dioxide and water using an external energy source. Heterotrophic organisms such as animals and fungi take in ready-made organic food from other organisms.

5.2 A balanced diet

A balanced diet supplies all seven classes of nutrient in the right proportions for a person's age, sex and activity.

Nutrient Main function Source
Carbohydrate Main energy supply Rice, bread, potato
Protein Growth and repair of tissue Fish, egg, beans
Lipid Energy store, insulation Oil, butter, nuts
Vitamin C Healthy gums, wound healing Citrus fruit
Vitamin D Calcium absorption Sunlight, oily fish
Calcium / iron Bones and teeth / haemoglobin Milk / liver, spinach
Fibre Aids peristalsis, prevents constipation Vegetables, wholegrain
Water Solvent and transport medium Drinks, food

Deficiency diseases follow from a missing nutrient: scurvy (vitamin C), rickets (vitamin D), anaemia (iron). Too much energy-rich food stored as fat leads to obesity and raises the risk of type-2 diabetes and heart disease.

5.3 The human digestive system

Food passes along the alimentary canal: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine (duodenum then ileum) → large intestine → rectum → anus. The liver and pancreas are associated organs that secrete into it. Muscular waves called peristalsis push food along.

5.4 Physical and chemical digestion

Physical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing it chemically — chewing by the teeth, churning by the stomach, and emulsification of fat by bile salts, which increases surface area for enzymes. Chemical digestion uses enzymes to hydrolyse large insoluble molecules into small soluble ones.

Region Enzyme Reaction
Mouth Salivary amylase Starch → maltose
Stomach Pepsin (acidic) Protein → polypeptides
Duodenum Pancreatic amylase, trypsin, lipase Starch, protein, fat broken down further
Ileum Maltase, peptidase, lipase Maltose → glucose; peptides → amino acids; fat → fatty acids + glycerol

Bile from the liver, stored in the gall bladder, is not an enzyme: it neutralises stomach acid to give pancreatic enzymes their working pH and emulsifies fats.

5.5 Absorption and assimilation

Absorption of digested food happens mainly in the ileum, whose wall is folded into millions of finger-like villi, each with microvilli, a rich blood capillary network and a central lacteal. These give a huge surface area and a short diffusion distance. Glucose and amino acids pass into the blood capillaries (by diffusion and active transport) and travel to the liver in the hepatic portal vein; fatty acids and glycerol enter the lacteals as fat droplets. Assimilation is the use of absorbed nutrients by cells — glucose for respiration, amino acids for new proteins.

Example. A villus is adapted for absorption by: a single layer of surface cells (short distance), microvilli (large area), many mitochondria (energy for active transport), and a dense blood supply (maintains a steep concentration gradient by carrying absorbed food away).

5.6 The large intestine and egestion

The colon reabsorbs most of the remaining water and mineral salts, leaving semi-solid faeces of fibre, dead cells and bacteria, stored in the rectum and removed through the anus as egestion. Egestion removes undigested material that never entered cells; it is different from excretion, which removes the waste products of metabolism.

5.7 Dental health and care of teeth

Humans have four types of tooth: incisors for cutting, canines for tearing, and premolars and molars for grinding. Each tooth has an enamel crown, a dentine layer, and a pulp cavity with nerves and blood vessels. Dental caries begins when bacteria in plaque ferment sugar to acid, which dissolves enamel; the decay then spreads into the dentine and reaches the pulp, causing pain. Reducing sugary snacks, brushing with fluoride toothpaste and regular check-ups prevent it. This links nutrition to the earlier idea that bacteria carry out the same enzyme-catalysed breakdown of sugars that our own cells do.

5.8 Photosynthesis: nutrition in green plants

Green plants trap light energy in chlorophyll and use it to combine carbon dioxide and water into glucose, releasing oxygen.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O →(light, chlorophyll)→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

The rate is limited by whichever factor is in shortest supply — light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. The glucose made is used in respiration, stored as starch, or built into cellulose, proteins and lipids. Plant and animal nutrition are linked: photosynthesis is the ultimate source of the food and oxygen that heterotrophs depend on.

5.9 Leaf structure and photosynthesis

The leaf is the plant's photosynthetic organ, and its structure fits the function. A broad, thin blade gives a large area for light capture and a short diffusion path for gases. The waxy cuticle and upper epidermis are transparent, letting light through to the palisade mesophyll, where most chloroplasts are. The spongy mesophyll below has air spaces connected to stomata on the lower surface, allowing carbon dioxide in and oxygen out. Veins of xylem bring water and phloem carries away the sugar made. Guard cells open and close the stomata to balance gas exchange against water loss.

Feeding Continuous, in light Intermittent meals

Deciding whether a described organism is an autotroph or a heterotroph, and identifying its energy and carbon sources, is a recurring exam task that draws the whole chapter together.
