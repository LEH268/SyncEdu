Chapter 4 — Metabolism and Enzymes

4.1 Metabolism

Metabolism is the sum of all chemical reactions in a living cell. It has two halves. Anabolism builds large molecules from small ones and requires energy — for example protein synthesis and photosynthesis. Catabolism breaks large molecules into small ones and releases energy — for example respiration and digestion. The two are linked: the energy released by catabolism, stored briefly as ATP, powers anabolism.

4.2 What an enzyme is

An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a protein that speeds up a specific reaction by lowering its activation energy, the energy barrier that must be crossed before reactants become products. The enzyme is not used up and is unchanged at the end, so a small amount works repeatedly. Enzyme names usually end in -ase and often name the substrate: sucrase acts on sucrose, lipase on lipids.

4.3 The lock-and-key model

Each enzyme has an active site — a region with a shape complementary to its substrate. The substrate fits into the active site like a key into a lock, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; the reaction occurs, and the products leave, freeing the site. Because the fit is specific, one enzyme catalyses only one reaction or one type of bond. The induced-fit model refines this: the active site changes shape slightly to grip the substrate.

enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products

4.4 Effect of temperature

As temperature rises from cold, reaction rate increases because molecules move faster and collide more often, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form. Rate peaks at the optimum temperature — about 37 °C for human enzymes. Above the optimum, heat vibrates the enzyme so violently that bonds holding the active site's shape break: the enzyme is denatured, the substrate no longer fits, and rate falls sharply to zero. Denaturation is permanent; simply cooling does not restore activity.

Example 1. Catalase from potato produces oxygen bubbles fastest at around 40 °C. Boiled potato produces none because the catalase is denatured.

4.5 Effect of pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which its active site holds its correct shape. Pepsin in the stomach works best near pH 2; salivary amylase near pH 7; pancreatic enzymes near pH 8. Moving away from the optimum, even by one or two units, alters the charges on the amino acids of the active site, changes its shape and slows the reaction; a large change denatures the enzyme.

4.6 Effect of substrate and enzyme concentration

With enzyme fixed, increasing substrate concentration raises the rate until every active site is working as fast as it can — the enzyme is saturated — after which the rate levels off. With substrate in excess, increasing enzyme concentration gives a proportional rise in rate because more active sites are available.

Example 2. Adding more yeast to a fixed volume of hydrogen peroxide speeds oxygen release proportionally; adding more peroxide to a fixed mass of yeast speeds it only until the yeast enzymes are saturated.

4.7 Enzyme inhibitors

A competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and blocks the active site; its effect is reduced by adding more substrate. A non-competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere, changing the active site's shape, and adding substrate does not help. Many drugs and poisons act as enzyme inhibitors.

4.8 Uses of enzymes

Field Enzyme use
Food industry Pectinase clarifies fruit juice; rennet clots milk for cheese
Detergents Protease and lipase remove food and blood stains at low temperature
Medicine Enzyme levels in blood diagnose heart and liver damage
Textiles Amylase removes starch coatings from woven cloth

4.9 Investigating enzyme activity

Rate is measured either as the time to reach an end point (for example starch fully digested, tested with iodine) or as the amount of product formed in a set time (for example volume of oxygen from catalase). A valid experiment controls temperature, pH, and the concentrations that are not being tested, and repeats readings to find a mean. Plotting rate against the changed factor and describing the shape — rising, peaking, then falling for temperature — is the key analytical skill of this chapter.

4.10 Reading a rate graph

When rate is calculated as 1 ÷ time, a graph of rate against temperature rises to a clear peak at the optimum and then drops steeply as denaturation sets in; a graph of rate against pH is a symmetrical hump centred on the optimum. For substrate concentration the curve rises then flattens into a plateau, and the plateau height rises if more enzyme is added. Being able to label the optimum, explain the rising part in terms of collisions, and explain the falling part in terms of denaturation earns the marks.

4.11 Immobilised enzymes

Enzymes fixed to an inert support such as alginate beads can be reused, do not contaminate the product, and are more stable to heat and pH. Lactose-free milk is made by passing milk over immobilised lactase. This industrial use depends on the same properties studied in the lab: specificity, sensitivity to conditions, and the fact that the enzyme is not consumed.

4.12 Why cells need enzymes at all

Without catalysis, the reactions of metabolism would run far too slowly at body temperature to keep a cell alive, and raising the temperature enough to speed them would denature the cell's proteins. Enzymes solve this by lowering activation energy so reactions proceed quickly at 37 °C, and because each is specific, the cell can switch individual pathways on and off by controlling which enzymes it makes.
