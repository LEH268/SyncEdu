Chapter 3 — Chemical Composition in a Cell

3.1 The elements of life

About 96% of a cell's mass is carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Smaller amounts of phosphorus, sulfur, calcium, potassium, sodium, chlorine, magnesium and iron, plus trace elements, complete the list. These atoms are joined into four main classes of organic molecule — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids — together with water and mineral ions.

3.2 Water

Water makes up 60–90% of a living cell. Its molecule is polar: the oxygen end is slightly negative, the hydrogen ends slightly positive, so water molecules attract one another and many solutes. This gives water its biological roles: a solvent in which reactions occur and substances are transported, a reactant in hydrolysis and photosynthesis, a coolant because much heat is needed to evaporate it, and a medium that gives cells support through turgor.

3.3 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen with hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2:1. They are the cell's main short-term energy source.

Group Examples Role
Monosaccharide Glucose, fructose, galactose Immediate energy; building block
Disaccharide Maltose, sucrose, lactose Transport form (sucrose in plants)
Polysaccharide Starch, glycogen, cellulose Storage (starch, glycogen); structure (cellulose)

Two monosaccharides join by a condensation reaction that removes one water molecule and forms a glycosidic bond; hydrolysis adds water to break the bond.

glucose + glucose → maltose + water

3.4 Proteins

Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and often sulfur. Their building blocks are amino acids, of which there are 20; each has an amino group, a carboxyl group and a variable R group. Amino acids join by peptide bonds formed in condensation reactions to make a polypeptide, which folds into a precise three-dimensional shape held by further bonds. That shape determines function.

Type Example Function
Structural Collagen, keratin Support in skin, tendon, hair
Enzymes Amylase, catalase Catalyse reactions
Transport Haemoglobin Carries oxygen
Hormonal Insulin Regulates blood glucose
Defence Antibodies Bind pathogens

Heat and extreme pH cause denaturation: the bonds holding the shape break, the protein unfolds, and it can no longer work. This is irreversible for most proteins.

3.5 Lipids

Lipids contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen but far less oxygen than carbohydrates, which is why they release more than twice as much energy per gram. A triglyceride is one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids by ester bonds, again by condensation. Saturated fatty acids have no carbon–carbon double bonds and are usually solid (animal fats); unsaturated ones have one or more double bonds and are usually liquid (plant oils). Phospholipids, with a phosphate group replacing one fatty acid, build membranes. Lipids also insulate, cushion organs and store energy long-term.

3.6 Nucleic acids

DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides, each nucleotide being a pentose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. DNA is a double helix carrying the genetic code; RNA is single-stranded and carries that code to the ribosomes for protein synthesis.

3.7 Food tests

Food substance Test Positive result
Starch Add iodine solution Blue-black
Reducing sugar Heat with Benedict's solution Green → yellow → brick-red
Protein Add Biuret reagent Violet / purple
Lipid Ethanol emulsion test White emulsion

Example. A food sample turns Benedict's solution brick-red and Biuret reagent violet, but iodine stays brown. It contains reducing sugar and protein but no starch — consistent with milk.

3.8 Condensation and hydrolysis as a common theme

All four macromolecules are assembled by condensation (monomers join, water is removed) and digested by hydrolysis (water is added, bonds break). Recognising this single pattern across carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids ties the chapter together and explains why digestion always needs water and enzymes.

3.9 Mineral ions and their roles

Although present in tiny amounts, mineral ions are essential. Nitrate ions supply the nitrogen a plant needs to make amino acids and proteins; magnesium ions are part of the chlorophyll molecule; iron is at the centre of haemoglobin; calcium hardens bone and is needed for blood clotting; iodine is built into thyroxine. A deficiency of any one produces a specific symptom — for example a magnesium-deficient plant shows yellowing between the leaf veins (chlorosis) because it cannot make enough chlorophyll.

3.10 Comparing the energy value of foods

Gram for gram, lipid releases about 38 kJ, while carbohydrate and protein each release about 17 kJ. This is why fat is the body's long-term energy store and why oily foods are described as energy-dense. In a simple calorimetry experiment a known mass of food is burned under a boiling tube of water; the temperature rise, the mass of water and its specific heat capacity give the energy released. Heat lost to the surroundings makes the measured value an underestimate — an important evaluation point.

3.11 Reading and interpreting food-test results

Examiners often give a table of results for several samples and ask which nutrients each contains, or which sample is a named food. The method is to take each test in turn, decide positive or negative from the colour, and combine the conclusions. A colour change that stops partway — Benedict's turning green rather than brick-red — indicates a small amount of reducing sugar rather than none, so the description of the colour matters, not just "positive" or "negative".
