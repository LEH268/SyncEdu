Chapter 2 — Movement of Substances Across the Plasma Membrane

2.1 The plasma membrane

The plasma membrane encloses every cell and controls what enters and leaves. It is described by the fluid mosaic model: a double layer of phospholipids, fluid because the molecules drift sideways, and a mosaic because proteins of many kinds are embedded in it. The phospholipid heads are hydrophilic and face the watery surroundings; the tails are hydrophobic and face inward. This arrangement makes the membrane partially permeable (semi-permeable): small, non-polar molecules pass freely, while large or charged particles need protein channels or carriers.

2.2 Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, until evenly spread. It is passive — it needs no energy from the cell because the particles' own kinetic energy drives it. Rate of diffusion increases with a steeper gradient, higher temperature, larger surface area, shorter distance and smaller particle size.

Example 1. In the lungs, oxygen diffuses from the alveolar air, where it is concentrated, across the thin moist wall into the blood, where it is scarce; carbon dioxide diffuses the opposite way. The large surface area of the alveoli and the one-cell-thick wall make this fast.

2.3 Osmosis

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: the net movement of water molecules across a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a concentrated solution (low water potential). Only water moves; the solute cannot cross. Water potential is highest in pure water and is lowered by adding solute.

2.4 Osmosis in animal cells

Animal cells have no cell wall, so water gain or loss changes their volume.

External solution Term Effect on red blood cell
More dilute than cell (hypotonic) Water enters Cell swells and bursts — haemolysis
Equal concentration (isotonic) No net movement No change
More concentrated than cell (hypertonic) Water leaves Cell shrinks and crinkles — crenation

2.5 Osmosis in plant cells

The cellulose cell wall resists expansion, so a plant cell in a dilute solution takes in water until the wall pushes back: it becomes firm, or turgid, and turgor pressure supports non-woody plants. In a concentrated solution the cell loses water, the vacuole shrinks, and the plant wilts; if water loss continues the cytoplasm pulls away from the wall — plasmolysis. A plasmolysed cell can often be recovered by placing it in water before permanent damage occurs.

Example 2. Potato strips left in strong salt solution become limp and lose mass because water leaves the cells by osmosis; strips in distilled water become stiff and gain mass.

2.6 Active transport

Active transport is the movement of particles against their concentration gradient, from low to high concentration, using energy as ATP and specific carrier proteins in the membrane. It stops if respiration is blocked (for example by lack of oxygen or by cyanide), which is the classic evidence that it is energy-dependent.

Example 3. Root hair cells absorb mineral ions such as nitrate from the soil even though the ions are already more concentrated inside the cell. Cells lining the small intestine use active transport to take up the last of the glucose after a meal.

2.7 Facilitated diffusion and comparison

Facilitated diffusion moves particles down their gradient but through a protein channel or carrier, allowing ions and glucose — which cannot cross the lipid layer — to enter passively.

What moves Any particle Water only Any particle
Direction Down gradient Down water potential Against gradient
Energy (ATP) No No Yes
Carrier protein Sometimes Via aquaporins Always

2.8 Surface area to volume ratio

As a cell or organism grows, volume increases faster than surface area, so the surface-area-to-volume ratio falls. A low ratio means exchange by diffusion alone becomes too slow to supply the interior. Small size, flattened shape, folded membranes (microvilli) and transport systems are all adaptations that keep exchange surfaces adequate. This principle links directly to gas exchange, absorption in the gut, and the need for a circulatory system in large animals.

2.9 Investigating osmosis

The standard experiment uses cylinders of potato or strips of visking tubing. Cylinders of equal size are weighed, placed in a range of sucrose concentrations, left for a fixed time, blotted and reweighed. The percentage change in mass is plotted against concentration. The concentration at which the graph crosses zero change is the point where the external solution has the same water potential as the cell contents — an isotonic point. Controlled variables are the size of the cylinder, temperature, time, and the type of tissue; the independent variable is the sucrose concentration.

Example 4. A potato cylinder gains 8% mass in distilled water, no change in 0.3 mol dm⁻³ sucrose, and loses 12% in 0.6 mol dm⁻³. Its cell sap is equivalent to about 0.3 mol dm⁻³ sucrose.

2.10 Where each process matters in the body

Process Example in the human body
Diffusion Gas exchange in the alveoli and at respiring tissues
Osmosis Water reabsorption in the kidney tubule and colon
Active transport Glucose and ion uptake in the ileum; ion reabsorption in the kidney
Facilitated diffusion Glucose entering red blood cells

Recognising which transport process a described situation involves — by checking the direction of movement, whether water alone moves, and whether energy is required — is the central skill this chapter builds toward.
